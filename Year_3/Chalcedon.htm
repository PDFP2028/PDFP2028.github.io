The Council of Chalcedon (AD 451) 

The Council of Chalcedon stands as one of the most pivotal moments in the entire history of Christian doctrine. Here is a thorough summary: 

📍 Historical Context 

The Chalcedonian Creed was adopted at the Council of Chalcedon in 451 in Asia Minor as a response to certain heretical views concerning the nature of Christ. Convoked by the Emperor Marcian, it was attended by about 520 bishops or their representatives and was the largest and best-documented of the early councils. 

While the first Council of Nicaea had established the eternal, pre-existent Godhead of Christ, the Symbol of the fourth ecumenical Council relates to the incarnate Logos, as He walked upon earth and sits at the right hand of the Father. 

⚔️ The Heresies Being Confronted 

The council was primarily a response to two dangerous errors about the person of Jesus Christ: 

1. Nestorianism is the view that after the Incarnation there were two persons — the divine and the human — united together by a common will. Nestorianism's error was the failure to unite the two natures in one person; each nature represented separate persons somehow possessed by the man Jesus. 

2. Eutychianism (Monophysitism) Chalcedon was principally attempting to respond to a new heretical view espoused by Archimandrite Eutyches from Constantinople, who proposed that in the Incarnation the divine and human substances somehow became "mixed" as a tertium quid (a third thing) — something more than human but less than divine. In other words, the teaching of Eutyches held that Christ had only one nature — a mixture of human and divine. 

✝️ The Chalcedonian Definition 

The most famous portion of its decree declares that Jesus, the "Lord, Only-begotten, is recognized in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation." 

These four famous "negatives" guard the truth from all four directions of error: 

·       Without confusion — refutes Eutyches (the natures are not blended) 

·       Without change — the natures are not altered by their union 

·       Without division — refutes Nestorius (there are not two separate persons) 

·       Without separation — Christ is one undivided Person 

The council approved the creed of Nicaea (325), the creed of Constantinople (381), two letters of St. Cyril of Alexandria against Nestorius which insisted on the unity of divine and human persons in Christ, and the Tome of Pope Leo I confirming two distinct natures in Christ and rejecting the monophysite doctrine. 

🧠 The Doctrine of the Hypostatic Union 

This doctrine is referred to as the doctrine of the "hypostatic union" because the two natures are united in the one person (hypostasis) of the Son. 

Even after the union, each nature retains its proper metaphysical attributes. The divine nature is not changed or altered in the hypostatic union, and the created human body and soul are not turned into something else by virtue of being united to the divine nature. The divine nature does not take on or acquire the attributes of created being, and the human nature does not take on or acquire the attributes of the Creator. 

This is the theological anchor that explains the entire Gospel: the same Jesus who wept at the tomb of Lazarus is the same Jesus who raised him from the dead. 

🌍 Significance & Reception 

The Council of Chalcedon (AD 451) is a landmark in the history of theology in the same way that the Council of Nicaea was. The definition produced by the council has been the standard of orthodox Christology ever since, not only in the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches but in Protestant churches as well. 

Besides reinforcing canons of earlier Church councils, the council also issued disciplinary decrees affecting monks and clergy, and declared Jerusalem and Constantinople patriarchates. 

💡 Why This Matters 

The Chalcedonian definition is not merely an academic exercise — it is salvifically essential. If Christ were not truly God, He could not save us. If He were not truly man, He could not represent us. The hypostatic union is the very foundation upon which the entire work of redemption rests.  As St. Athanasius articulated: God became man so that man might become God — and Chalcedon guards that truth with precision and love.
